The Structural Framework of Iran's Digital Repression
and Network Architecture
The landscape of information control in the Islamic Republic of Iran has undergone a profound
transformation, shifting from localized, reactive kinetic interventions to a continuous, multi-tiered
digital repression infrastructure. Historically, the state's efforts during periods of civil unrest, such
as the Green Movement of 2009, relied on sporadic filtering and visible security deployments.
However, as demonstrated during the nationwide protests that erupted on December 28, 2025,
and the subsequent external military escalations of February 28, 2026, the state has
institutionalized centralized command structures designed to maintain absolute control over the
domestic and international information ecosystems.
This system is overseen by the Supreme Council for Cyberspace, a central policy-making body
established in 2012 under the direct oversight of the Supreme Leader. The Council’s decisions
are operationalized by the National Cyberspace Center, which coordinates enforcement across
various state organs, including the Cyber Police Force (FATA). Established in 2011, FATA
actively monitors the digital sphere with a mandate to suppress political dissent and prosecute
activities deemed contrary to state-defined religious and moral values.
The technical foundation of this system is the National Information Network (NIN), a
state-managed domestic internet ecosystem initiated around 2010. The NIN’s decentralized
architecture allows the state to implement graduated network responses. During periods of
heightened domestic tension or external threat, authorities can execute blanket shutdowns of
the global internet while keeping domestic services—including banking, state databases, and
approved domestic messaging applications—fully online.
This dual capability was demonstrated on January 8, 2026, when the state imposed a
nationwide shutdown of internet, phone, and mobile networks amid escalating protests. This
communication blackout coincided with the killing of at least 34 people and the arrest of over
2,200 individuals, including students, journalists, and human rights defenders. By cutting off
global connectivity, the state prevented the documentation and real-time reporting of human
rights violations.
The ultimate operational test of this infrastructure occurred on February 28, 2026, during military
strikes launched by the United States and Israel. Authorities reduced international bandwidth
connectivity to a mere 1 percent of ordinary levels, effectively isolating 92 million citizens from
external news sources.

During these nationwide blackouts, the state employs a selective connectivity model, granting
filtered internet access to approved state actors through special credentials, colloquially referred
to as "White SIM cards". This selective system allows state-aligned figures to promote official
narratives internationally while independent and dissenting voices remain completely offline. For
example, during the February 2026 wartime blackout, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi
conducted a live, unfiltered Zoom interview with CBS, illustrating how the state utilizes selective
connectivity to manage international perceptions while isolating its civilian population.
The international watch dog group Article 19, a UK-based human rights organization founded in
1986, has consistently documented these censorship practices. It advocates for the protection
of fundamental rights under the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International
Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR). Through its regional monitoring, Article 19 has
highlighted how repressive legal frameworks, national security laws, and state-imposed
blackouts in Iran, Saudi Arabia, and Egypt are systematically utilized to silence civil society and
target vulnerable communities, including women and LGBTQI individuals.
Domestic Platform Migration as a Vector for
Surveillance, Moral Policing, and Narrative
Monopolization
To reinforce its network boundaries, the state has pursued a policy of forced migration, driving
citizens off secure global communication platforms and onto state-controlled domestic
alternatives. By maintaining blocks on international services like Instagram, the state has left
citizens with no practical choice but to use domestic platforms such as Eitaa, Rubika, Bale, and
Shad for daily communication, business, and education. This forced migration integrates state
surveillance and censorship directly into the daily lives of the population.

The structural unification of this domestic app ecosystem is achieved through the Message
Exchange Bus (MXB). The MXB is an interoperability protocol designed to connect Eitaa, Bale,
Soroush, Rubika, Gap, and iGap, allowing users to exchange files, messages, and calls across
platforms without separate accounts. While presented as a technological convenience, the MXB
functions as a data centralization tool, consolidating communication data and metadata into
unified monitoring streams.
A technical assessment conducted by the security lab of the Open Technology Fund (OTF)
revealed that Eitaa, Rubika, and Bale lack basic security protocols and compromise user
privacy. These applications require extensive device permissions, and users have reported that
platforms like Rubika can access and scan local device galleries. Due to these security
vulnerabilities and surveillance links, these apps were removed from Google Play and the Apple
App Store, leaving users dependent on local repositories like Bazaar and Myket.
Censorship and behavioral modification are actively enforced within these domestic platforms.
Small businesses that rely on Rubika or Bale for marketing face high advertising fees and strict
moral policing. For example, Rubika charges business owners up to 63 million tomans
(approximately $359) for a 15-minute promotional slot. However, these promotions must
undergo a lengthy, highly conservative approval process. Advertisements are frequently rejected
for minor perceived infractions, such as a female business owner's elbow being visible for a few
seconds or the promotion of female undergarments.
This moral policing is mirrored in the state-sanctioned search engine Zarebin, which filters
search queries to omit commercial listings for female undergarments while returning standard
results for male equivalents. In the education sector, the Shad platform is structurally configured
to limit student interaction. The application restricts communication so that only the teacher can
speak during designated hours, blocking student feedback and questions until late afternoon.
Furthermore, these domestic networks serve as channels for political censorship and state
propaganda. Accounts that use historical national symbols, such as the pre-1979 Lion and Sun
emblem, or profiles featuring portraits of Mohammad Reza Pahlavi, are systematically banned.
Concurrently, these platforms are used to circulate coordinated disinformation. During the 2026
military crisis, state-linked channels used Rubika and Bale to spread false reports, including
claims of an absolute military victory over the United States and Israel, and the false death of
Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. This controlled information environment aims to
project an image of the state as a land of "peace, friendship, and human rights" while
suppressing the reality of the domestic crisis.
State-Sponsored Hate Speech and Systematic
Persecution of the Baha'i Religious Minority
A critical element of domestic narrative control is the systematic, state-sponsored
dehumanization of marginalized groups, most notably the Baha'i community, which represents
Iran's largest non-Muslim religious minority. Unrecognized by the constitution of the Islamic
Republic, Baha'is are denied fundamental civil rights, access to higher education, and public
employment. Following the national protests of January 2026 and the subsequent military
escalations in February 2026, the state has intensified its targeting of the Baha'is, treating them
as internal enemies of the state to divert public anger and justify authoritarian crackdowns.
The current crackdown is characterized by a high degree of state coordination, involving
arbitrary detentions, violent home raids, properties being seized, and incommunicado detentions
that may amount to enforced disappearances. Between June and November 2025, the Baha'i
International Community (BIC) documented over 750 distinct persecutory acts across Iran—a
threefold increase compared to the same period in 2024. This wave has continued to escalate
into mid-2026, with sweep operations targeting families in Mashhad, Shiraz, Borujerd, Aligudarz,
and Semnan.
Baha'i business owners have had their workplaces sealed, and gravesites belonging to the
community have been systematically desecrated. This systematic campaign is designed to
induce terror, isolate the community, and enforce collective punishment under fabricated
charges such as "propagating against Sharia Islam" or "forming groups to act against national
security".
To validate these physical crackdowns, the state orchestrates pervasive online and offline hate
speech. Since 2017, more than 33,000 pieces of anti-Baha'i content have been broadcast or
published across state radio, television, newspapers, and social media platforms. Data collected
during key monitoring periods, such as between November 2020 and April 2021, demonstrated
a 36 percent increase in state-sponsored hate content compared to previous quarters, indicating
a calculated escalation in propaganda. The state actively utilizes manipulated and desecrated
imagery to cultivate public hostility, such as the public distortion of "the Greatest Name," a highly
sacred Baha'i symbol equivalent to the Holy Cross in Christianity or the word Allah in Islam.
Another common technique involves superimposing false, occultist, or cult-like imagery over
photographs of the Shrine of Bahá'u'lláh to frame the peaceful community as a dangerous cult.
Because Baha'is are strictly denied the right of reply in any state-controlled or domestic media,
these narratives go completely unchallenged within the domestic space, allowing the regime to
systematically build a hostile environment that serves to justify ongoing judicial abuses, lengthy
prison sentences, and asset confiscation. Baha'i women face a dual layer of vulnerability; they
are disproportionately targeted for their participation in civil rights movements and are subjected
to harsher judicial sentences due to the intersection of gender-based repression and religious
discrimination.

United Nations Special Rapporteur on Freedom of Expression. The research shows that women

Computational Natural Language Processing and
Farsi Hate Speech Datasets
The automated identification of hate speech and incitement to violence in the Iranian digital
ecosystem presents significant computational challenges. Farsi remains a low-resource
language (LRL) in the domain of Natural Language Processing (NLP). The development of
robust, automated models is historically hindered by a severe scarcity of high-quality, annotated
public datasets, combined with the complex linguistic nuances of Farsi, such as code-mixing,
colloquialisms, and highly metaphorical or uncivil rhetorical structures.

These datasets highlight the challenge of class imbalance in Farsi hate speech detection. In the
Pars-OFF dataset, for instance, only 10 percent of offensive tweets are labeled as untargeted at
Level B. To address this imbalance, researchers deploy preprocessing pipelines that utilize
oversampling techniques (such as 50 percent, 75 percent, and 100 percent random duplication
of the minority class) to prevent model bias and ensure accurate classification.
The choice of machine learning architecture is critical for detection accuracy. Classical machine
learning approaches (such as Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Naive Bayes utilizing
TF-IDF representations) struggle with semantic depth, irony, sarcasm, and cross-sentence
contexts. To overcome these limitations, fine-tuned, encoder-only transformer models have
been introduced. Models such as ParsBERT, which are pre-trained specifically on Farsi corpora,
have demonstrated significant performance advantages.
Comparative evaluations show that ParsBERT significantly outperforms decoder-only models,
including multiple ChatGPT model families, in identifying Farsi hate speech. This discrepancy
arises because generic large language models (LLMs) struggle to parse the subtle, rhetorical
forms of Farsi incivility and distinguish them from general profanity. In Farsi digital spaces,
uncivil messages often contain no explicit vulgar terms, relying instead on metaphorical
constructs to attack individuals or communities.
In addressing these challenges, research shows that changing the language of the prompt
(such as prompting in English versus Farsi) has no remarkable effect on ChatGPT's analytical
output, pointing to a fundamental limitation in the underlying training representations of
general-purpose LLMs when dealing with Farsi. To improve detection, researchers classify Farsi
incivility into three distinct categories: Pejorative Speech (PS), Insults, and Threatening
Messages (TMs). This taxonomy allows fine-tuned models to distinguish between general
profanity and targeted, harmful speech, providing a more robust framework for automated
monitoring.
Open-Source Intelligence Frameworks and Actionable
De-escalation Methodologies
The conversion of raw social media data and online chatter into actionable intelligence requires
a structured Open Source Intelligence (OSINT) framework. An OSINT framework provides a
repeatable, disciplined pipeline designed to process vast amounts of unstructured, public data
while bypassing the challenges of information overload, fragmented data sources, and accuracy
errors. This pipeline operates through the standard stages of the intelligence cycle:

To distinguish hate speech from offensive language, OSINT frameworks must employ rigorous
diagnostic models. A notable development in this domain is the xList-Hate framework. Rather
than prompting a language model to make a binary, black-box decision on whether a text
constitutes hate speech, xList-Hate decomposes the evaluation into a checklist of ten explicit,
concept-level binary questions grounded in legal and policy standards (such as targeting of
protected groups, dehumanization, and incitement to violence).
These diagnostic signals are then aggregated by a lightweight, fully interpretable decision tree.
Let H(x) represent the final classification of a text x as hate speech, determined by a mapping
function f applied to the set of ten binary diagnostic factors q_i \in \{0, 1\}:
where the function f is represented by a transparent, auditable decision tree. This methodology
separates semantic judgment from classification logic, making the output robust to distribution
shifts and contextual ambiguity, and highly suitable for human rights monitoring.
At the international level, this diagnostic approach aligns with the Rabat Plan of Action, a
comprehensive threshold test adopted by the United Nations to evaluate whether speech
constitutes incitement to discrimination, hostility, or violence. The Rabat Plan of Action outlines
six distinct factors that must be evaluated:
● Social Context: The prevailing political, social, and economic climate surrounding the
speech.
● Speaker Status: The position, influence, and authority of the speaker relative to the
target audience.
● Intent: The deliberate intent to advocate hatred or incite violence against a protected
group.
● Content and Form: The style, tone, and specific rhetorical devices employed in the
speech.
● Reach and Extent: The audience size, frequency of communication, and dissemination
channels.
● Imminence of Harm: The direct causal likelihood that the speech will result in real-world
violence or discrimination.
This framework is highly relevant to international human rights monitoring. Under Article 19(3) of
the ICCPR, states may restrict freedom of expression to protect national security, public order,
or the rights of others. However, such restrictions must be provided by law that is precise and
public, and they must meet strict tests of necessity and proportionality.
The UN Special Rapporteur on Freedom of Expression has highlighted how counter-terrorism
and national security laws are frequently misused by states to suppress legitimate dissent and
target minority groups. The Special Rapporteur's findings indicate a global surge in hate speech
and disinformation, with social media platforms like Facebook, Telegram, and particularly X
serving as major vectors during polarized conflicts. While international criminal law prohibits
direct and public incitement to genocide, distinguishing between offensive speech and actual
incitement requires the rigorous contextual analysis outlined in the Rabat Plan.
The predictive link between digital speech and real-world violence has been empirically
demonstrated by pioneering organizations such as HateLab. Merging criminology with machine
learning, HateLab’s methodology tracks hate speech in real-time by mapping "breadcrumb
trails" (temporal, geographic, and network dispersion patterns).
Their empirical research has established a direct predictive correlation, demonstrating that
localized spikes in online hate speech are reliably followed by physical hate crimes in the same
geographic areas. Building on this foundation, the spin-out platform Nisien.ai has integrated
these capabilities into its Hero Platform.
The platform features a multi-tiered defense architecture:
● Hero Detect: Uses AI models to map language patterns and identify rising societal
tensions or emerging harmful narratives.
● Hero Defend: Allows administrators to protect digital environments by hiding or removing
identified toxic content.
● Hero Resolve: Leverages generative AI to suggest de-escalation responses. Rather than
relying on heavy-handed censorship, the platform suggests empathy-inducing
counter-narratives grounded in behavioral science to persuade individuals to alter their
behavior. This resolution capability has been shown to reduce negative online interactions
by up to 65 percent in testing environments, preserving free speech while mitigating
real-world harm.
Actionable OSINT Recommendations and Strategic
De-escalation Initiatives
To counter state-sponsored hate speech and digital repression in the Iranian information
ecosystem, civil society organizations, international human rights monitors, and technology
developers should implement targeted, technical de-escalation strategies.
Decentralized OSINT Data Collection Pipelines
Because the Iranian state utilizes the National Information Network to execute global internet
shutdowns, monitoring organizations must build decentralized data collection systems. These
systems should deploy a distributed network of local proxies and virtual private networks to
maintain connectivity during bandwidth throttling events.
OSINT collection pipelines must prioritize the secure ingestion of data from domestic
applications such as Eitaa, Rubika, and Bale, which have become the primary channels for
forced migration. To protect local collectors, these pipelines should employ containerized,
virtualized environments that isolate application data, preventing domestic platforms from
accessing local hardware identifiers or scanning device galleries. Collected data should be
continuously streamed to secure, off-shore servers for real-time analysis.
Localized NLP Classification Models
To identify and analyze Farsi hate speech, monitoring organizations should deploy fine-tuned,
localized encoder models rather than relying on general-purpose LLMs. Automated detection
systems should utilize ParsBERT, pre-trained on Farsi corpora, to effectively identify the subtle,
non-insulting rhetorical incivility common in Farsi digital spaces.
These models should be integrated with diagnostic frameworks like xList-Hate, decomposing
hate speech evaluation into explicit, concept-level binary questions rather than relying on direct
black-box classification. Preprocessing pipelines must incorporate oversampling techniques
(such as 100 percent random duplication of minority classes) to address severe class
imbalances in Farsi datasets, ensuring accurate classification across targeted groups.
AI-Driven Behavioral Counter-Speech
To mitigate online hate speech without relying on heavy-handed censorship, organizations
should deploy automated, generative AI assistance tools that suggest empathy-inducing
counter-narratives based on Nisien.ai's "Resolve" methodology. These de-escalation tools
should be integrated into communication channels used by activists and human rights
defenders, providing real-time, behaviorally validated responses to hostile online actors.
The suggested counter-speech must be culturally and linguistically tailored to Farsi, directly
addressing the underlying anxieties exploited by state-sponsored propaganda. By utilizing
empathy-inducing counter-narratives, monitoring groups can de-escalate digital conflicts and
reduce negative online interactions, preserving free speech while mitigating real-world harm.
International Regulatory Pressure and Technical Accountability
International human rights organizations should leverage documented OSINT findings to
pressure global digital intermediaries and international regulatory bodies. Organizations must
document state-imposed internet blackouts as systematic violations of Articles 19 and 21 of the
ICCPR, submitting comprehensive reports to the United Nations Special Rapporteur on
Freedom of Expression to support targeted sanctions against the leadership of the Supreme
Council for Cyberspace.
Concurrently, civil society should coordinate with global application stores to block the
distribution of state-backed domestic apps, such as Eitaa and Rubika, that lack basic security
protocols and actively compromise user privacy. By restricting state access to global distribution
channels, international actors can increase the political cost of digital repression and protect the
digital rights of the civilian population.
